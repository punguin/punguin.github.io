At Zenni, design mostly lived in Figma, and the reasoning behind it mostly lived in meetings. I proposed a lightweight way to work in the open: UX working docs in Slack canvases, built from a template I made. Teams started using it to house their own work.

<dl class="case-overview">
<div><dt>The problem</dt><dd>Figma showed what we designed, not why. Decisions were often made on the surface, the work of understanding a problem was often skipped, and unless you were in the meeting, you likely had no visibility into a tradeoff or a decision.</dd></div>
<div><dt>My role</dt><dd>I saw the gap and spearheaded the fix, for the UX team and for our partners. I proposed the workflow, created the canvas template, and led my own projects in it first. Later I co-facilitated a workshop with a UX colleague so the team could shape its knowledge sharing mechanism together.</dd></div>
<div><dt>What I did</dt><dd>A lightweight UX working doc workflow that lived in Slack, the tool everyone was already in: one canvas per project where the purpose, the understanding of the problem, the decisions and their tradeoffs were written down and open for anyone to read and comment on.</dd></div>
</dl>

#### 03 / Understanding the problem

Design lived in Figma. The thinking lived in meetings.

##### 3.1 People

<p class="case-insight">If you weren’t in the meeting, you didn’t see the decision.</p>

Designers, product managers, engineers and business partners all depended on design decisions, but only the people in the room saw how they were made. Everyone else saw the result in Figma, without the tradeoffs that led to it, and couldn’t weigh in until the decision was already made.

##### 3.2 System

<p class="case-insight">Figma holds the what. Nothing held the why.</p>

Figma was where design happened, and it was good at showing screens. It wasn’t built to hold the problem a screen was solving, the options we considered, or why we picked one. There was no existing mechanism to track decisions or collaborate on them. Slack, meanwhile, was where everyone already worked.

##### 3.3 Gaps

<p class="case-insight">Decisions were made on the surface.</p>

Without a place to write down the understanding, it was easy to skip it and go straight to the screens. The quality of decisions suffered, and so did visibility.

#### 04 / Making it work

A lightweight template in the tool we already used.

##### 4.1 Put the work where people already are

No new tool, no new habit to learn first.

I proposed a lightweight UX working documentation workflow that lived in Slack. A canvas sits next to the conversations a team is already having, so it can be shared with a link and found later. Partners didn’t need a Figma seat or a meeting invite to follow along.

An added benefit was the threaded conversations. Anyone could comment directly on a tenet, a tradeoff or a decision, and the loop was closed right there in Slack, in a thread everyone could see.

I led my own projects this way first. On <a href="/work/zenni-account/">Zenni Account</a>, the canvases became where the decisions happened, and where anyone could see what was decided and why before it was final.

##### 4.2 Make understanding part of the template

The template asked for the thinking before the screens.

I turned those canvases into a template the team could adopt. The idea was to give just enough structure to show the tradeoffs and decisions, and no more. The Order Tracking working doc shows the shape:

- **Who and where.** The UX designer, content designer, product managers and TPM, with links to the Figma handoff and the team’s Slack channel.
- **Purpose and background.** One line on what the doc is for, and how the work connects to the squad’s vision.
- **Tenets.** The principles we used in every decision, “until we know better”, such as “Deliver confidence instantly” and “No dead ends, always provide value”.
- **Metrics.** The squad’s north star, and the goal for this initiative.
- **The work.** A project tracker, the messaging framework, an iteration tracker with a dated, recorded walkthrough for each iteration, user testing, and MVP scope and tradeoffs.

Putting the purpose, background and tenets first made the understanding part of a problem hard to skip.

##### 4.3 Let the team shape it

A mechanism only works if the team owns it.

Teams adopted the template and structured it however they needed. When the question grew from documenting projects to how the whole UX team shares knowledge, I co-facilitated a workshop with a UX colleague to co-build a knowledge sharing mechanism with the team, instead of handing one down.

The workshop was planned in a working doc of its own, with its goal, agenda and a tracker for everything we needed to prepare. Its goal was to collaboratively uncover knowledge gaps, find their root causes, and prioritize the most critical inefficiencies. We introduced what a mechanism is, a tool plus adoption, inspection and iteration, then collected pain points, dug into the top ones with Five Whys, and sorted them into major time sinks and quick wins.

##### Writing to bring people along

The same habit, on a bigger question.

Working in prose wasn’t only for projects. When the D2C organization restructured around delivery speed, I wrote a discussion starter for the UX team and D2C leaders on organizing designers by strategic domain instead. It said up front what it was, a discussion starter, and what it was not, a proposal to throw away our current structure. Peer reviewers added their thinking directly in the doc. Writing the argument out, in the open, is how I bring people along before asking them to agree.

#### 05 / What it enabled

Decisions anyone could find, and a team that kept the practice.

The working docs outlasted the project they started on. Later projects started from the template, and the team had a shared place to see what was decided and why.
